Unsupervised Learning

Cluster Analysis & Major Challenges

Cluster Analysis: Partitioning unlabeled objects into groups (clusters) such that intra-cluster similarity is maximized (points within a cluster are very close) and inter-cluster similarity is minimized (distinct clusters are far apart). No prior class labels exist.

Key Requirements of Clustering in Data Mining

1. Scalability: Handle millions of tuples ($O(n)$ or $O(n \log n)$ time) without memory breakdown.
2. Different Attribute Types: Work on numeric, nominal, binary, ordinal, and mixed data types.
3. Arbitrary Shapes: Discover non-convex, elongated, nested, or intertwined clusters (not just spherical blobs).
4. Noise Robustness: Isolate outliers and missing values without distorting genuine cluster centers.
5. High Dimensionality: Resist curse of dimensionality where distance metrics lose discriminating power.
6. Interpretability: Cluster boundaries and meanings must be easily understandable and actionable by domain experts.

Cluster Types

Cluster TypeCore ConceptExample Algorithm
Center-Based (Prototype)Points assigned to nearest prototype (mean or medoid). Spherical.K-Means, K-Medoids (PAM)
Density-BasedDense areas separated by sparse regions; arbitrary shapes & noise removal.DBSCAN, OPTICS, DENCLUE
HierarchicalTree of nested clusters (dendrogram) built agglomeratively or divisively.AGNES, DIANA, BIRCH
Grid-BasedQuantizes space into finite grid cells for fast spatial query processing.STING, CLIQUE

Partitioning

K-Means Algorithm & Objective Function

Sum of Squared Errors (SSE)

K-Means iteratively partitions $n$ points into $k$ clusters to minimize the overall SSE objective function:

$$\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{x \in C_i} \|x - \mu_i\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{x \in C_i} d(x, \mu_i)^2$$

where $\mu_i = \frac{1}{|C_i|}\sum_{x \in C_i} x$ is the mean centroid of cluster $C_i$, and Euclidean distance is $d(x,y) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_j - y_j)^2}$.

Algorithm Steps & Complexity

  1. Initialize: Arbitrarily select $k$ objects from dataset $D$ as initial cluster centroids $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k$.
  2. Assignment: Assign each object $x$ to its nearest centroid based on Euclidean distance: $x \in C_i$ if $d(x, \mu_i) = \min_j d(x, \mu_j)$.
  3. Update: Recompute each centroid $\mu_i$ as the arithmetic mean of all points assigned to $C_i$.
  4. Repeat: Iterate steps 2 and 3 until convergence (centroids no longer move or SSE change $< \epsilon$).
Time Complexity: $O(n \cdot k \cdot t)$ where $n = \text{points}$, $k = \text{clusters}$, $t = \text{iterations}$. Linear in $n$!
Space Complexity: $O(n + k)$ storage needed for data and centroids.

Strengths & Critical Limitations

STRENGTHS
• Fast, simple, and intuitive
• Scales linearly to very large datasets
• Guaranteed convergence to a local minimum
LIMITATIONS
• $k$ must be predetermined upfront
• Sensitive to random initial seed selection
• Highly distorted by outliers / noise
• Restricted to spherical / convex clusters of similar size

Variants & Diagnostics

K-Medoids, Categorical Extensions & Choosing K

K-Means Family Variants

VariantCore MechanismAdvantage / Purpose
K-Medoids (PAM)Uses actual data points (medoids) instead of virtual means. Swaps points to reduce absolute error.Highly robust to outliers and extreme noise. Time: $O(k(n-k)^2)$.
K-MediansCalculates dimension-wise median instead of mean for centers ($L_1$ norm).Resistant to outliers; handles skewed distributions.
K-ModesReplaces means with modes (most frequent categories) and uses simple matching dissimilarity.Extends clustering directly to categorical / nominal data.
K-PrototypesCombines K-Means Euclidean distance for numeric features + K-Modes dissimilarity for categorical features.Handles mixed datasets seamlessly without forced encodings.
Bisecting K-MeansStarts with 1 cluster; recursively splits the cluster with largest SSE using 2-means until $k$ clusters exist.Less sensitive to random initial seed; forms hierarchical dendrogram.
Mini-Batch K-MeansSamples small random subsets (mini-batches) to update centroids incrementally.Huge speedup on big data with negligible loss in cluster quality.

Determining Optimal $k$

1. Elbow Method

Plot total $\text{SSE}$ against increasing $k$. $\text{SSE}$ monotonically drops as $k \to n$. Look for the distinct inflection point ("elbow") where SSE reduction sharply levels off.

2. Silhouette Coefficient

For point $i$, $s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max(a(i), b(i))}$, where $a(i)$ is mean distance to points in same cluster, and $b(i)$ is mean distance to nearest neighboring cluster. Range is $[-1, +1]$; values close to $+1$ indicate outstanding clustering.

Hierarchical Clustering — Dendrograms & Linkages

Agglomerative vs Divisive

AspectAgglomerative (AGNES) — Bottom-UpDivisive (DIANA) — Top-Down
Starting PointEach object starts as its own individual singleton cluster ($n$ clusters).All objects start grouped together in a single grand cluster ($1$ cluster).
OperationSuccessively merge the two closest clusters at each step.Successively split clusters into smaller sub-clusters recursively.
TerminationStops when all points merge into 1 cluster or distance threshold met.Stops when each cluster holds 1 point or threshold met.
Complexity$O(n^2)$ space, $O(n^2 \log n)$ or $O(n^3)$ time. Irreversible merges.Computationally prohibitive without heuristics ($2^{n-1}-1$ possible splits).

Cluster Linkage Methods

LinkageDefinition & FormulaBehavior & Trade-offs
Single Linkage (MIN)$d(A, B) = \min_{x \in A, y \in B} d(x, y)$Can handle non-elliptical shapes; prone to undesirable chaining effect (thin bridges connect distinct groups).
Complete Linkage (MAX)$d(A, B) = \max_{x \in A, y \in B} d(x, y)$Produces compact, spherical clusters with equal diameters; highly sensitive to outliers.
Average Linkage (UPGMA)$d(A, B) = \frac{1}{|A||B|} \sum_{x \in A} \sum_{y \in B} d(x, y)$Balanced compromise between Single and Complete; robust to noise and outliers.
Ward's MethodMerges pair that minimizes increase in total within-cluster SSE.Favors spherical, equally sized clusters; widely used in social sciences and biology.

Density-Based

DBSCAN — Density-Based Spatial Clustering

DBSCAN: Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise. Discovers clusters of arbitrary shapes by connecting points situated in high-density regions separated by sparse noise zones.

Core Parameters & Point Classifications

Parameter / ConceptDefinitionRole in DBSCAN
$\epsilon$ (Eps)Maximum radius of the neighborhood around any point.Defines spatial locality $N_\epsilon(p) = \{q \in D \mid d(p,q) \le \epsilon\}$.
$\text{MinPts}$Minimum number of points within $\epsilon$-neighborhood to form dense region.Density threshold. Default heuristic: $\text{MinPts} \ge 2 \times \text{dim}$.
Core Point$|N_\epsilon(p)| \ge \text{MinPts}$Forms dense cluster interior; expands cluster to neighbors.
Border Point$|N_\epsilon(p)| < \text{MinPts}$, but $p \in N_\epsilon(\text{Core})$Resides on cluster periphery; belongs to cluster but cannot expand it.
Noise PointNeither Core nor BorderOutlier; does not belong to any cluster.

Density Reachability vs Connectivity

RelationFormal ConditionSymmetry Property
Directly Density-Reachable$p \in N_\epsilon(q)$ and $q$ is a Core point.Asymmetric (cannot reach core from non-core).
Density-ReachableChain of core points $p_1, \dots, p_n$ where $p_{i+1}$ is directly reachable from $p_i$.Asymmetric.
Density-ConnectedBoth $p$ and $q$ are density-reachable from some common core point $o$.Symmetric & Transitive (defines cluster).

Anomaly Detection

Outliers, Novelty Detection & IQR Fences

Noise vs Outlier (Vital Exam Distinction):
• Noise: Random error or measurement variance. Uninteresting garbage to be smoothed and removed in preprocessing.
• Outlier: Legitimate, rare, exceptional data objects generated by a different underlying mechanism. Highly interesting for fraud detection, network intrusion, and fault diagnosis!

Taxonomy of Outlier Types

Outlier TypeDefinitionReal-World Example
Global (Point) OutlierAn individual data point deviates significantly from the entire dataset.A monthly salary of $1,000,000 when average is $4,000.
Contextual (Conditional)Object is anomalous in a specific context (judged against behavioral attributes).$30^\circ\text{C}$ temperature in Antarctica during winter.
Collective OutlierA collection of objects is anomalous as a group, though individual points appear normal.Repeated burst of $1 transactions triggering credit card alert.

Outlier Detection Approaches & IQR Method

IQR (Interquartile Range) identifies outliers without assuming a normal distribution:

$$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1, \quad \text{Lower Fence} = Q_1 - 1.5 \times \text{IQR}, \quad \text{Upper Fence} = Q_3 + 1.5 \times \text{IQR}$$
Worked Example: Data: $10, 10, 11, 11, 12, 12, 13, 95$.
$Q_1 = 10.5, \, Q_3 = 12.5, \, \text{IQR} = 12.5 - 10.5 = 2.0$.
Lower Fence $= 10.5 - 1.5(2) = 7.5$; Upper Fence $= 12.5 + 1.5(2) = 15.5$.
Since $95 > 15.5$, it is flagged as an outlier! (Note: a $3\sigma$ test fails here because 95 inflates $\sigma$).

Information Filtering

Recommender Systems — Content-Based & Collaborative

Recommendation Paradigms

ParadigmCore PhilosophyKey AdvantageCritical Limitation
Content-BasedRecommends items similar to those previously liked by user (using item tags, genres).User-independent; no cold-start for new items; explainable.Overspecialization (no serendipitous discovery); needs rich tags.
Collaborative Filtering (CF)Leverages collective behavior ("users who agreed in the past will agree now").Domain-free; uncovers novel interests without feature engineering.Cold-start for new users/items; rating matrix sparsity.
Hybrid SystemsBlends Content-Based and CF models (e.g., Netflix, Spotify).Eases cold-start and sparsity significantly.Increased computational complexity and pipeline engineering.

User-Based Collaborative Filtering Formulation

Cosine similarity between user $u$ and user $v$ across co-rated item set $I_{uv}$:

$$\text{sim}(u, v) = \frac{\sum_{i \in I_{uv}} r_{ui} \cdot r_{vi}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i \in I_{uv}} r_{ui}^2} \cdot \sqrt{\sum_{i \in I_{uv}} r_{vi}^2}}$$

Predicted rating for user $u$ on unrated item $j$ using $k$-nearest neighbors:

$$\hat{r}_{uj} = \frac{\sum_{v \in N(u)} \text{sim}(u, v) \cdot r_{vj}}{\sum_{v \in N(u)} |\text{sim}(u, v)|}$$
Worked Example: Predict Asha's rating for Movie 3.
$\text{sim}(\text{Asha}, \text{Ben}) = 0.999$, $\text{sim}(\text{Asha}, \text{Dev}) = 0.47$. Ben rates M3 $= 5$, Dev rates M3 $= 2$.
$$\hat{r}_{\text{Asha}, M3} = \frac{0.999(5) + 0.47(2)}{0.999 + 0.47} = \frac{4.995 + 0.94}{1.469} \approx \mathbf{4.04} \quad (\text{Recommend!})$$
